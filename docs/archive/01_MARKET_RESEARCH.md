# Adaptive Core — Market Research

**Scope:** Qualitative discovery captured in the source conversation  
**Market:** Global consumer mobile fitness  
**Initial niche:** Short, adaptive, at-home core training

## 1. Research question

Can a core-training product win in a crowded market by replacing fixed schedules with a genuine, explainable performance-to-next-workout loop?

The discovery work was used to define a product direction, not to produce a statistically representative market-size study.

## 2. Evidence discipline

Three evidence classes are used throughout this document:

- **Observed example:** a complaint or behavior was directly reported in an app-store/community example discussed during discovery.
- **Recurring qualitative theme:** similar complaints appeared across multiple examples or product categories.
- **Product hypothesis:** a conclusion that must be tested with Adaptive Core users.

The source conversation explicitly stated that the work was not a 10,000- or 20,000-review dataset. Download counts, market size, precise complaint percentages, and current competitor prices were not reliably established. They must not be quoted as validated facts.

## 3. Market structure

### Fixed challenge applications

These products typically offer beginner/intermediate/advanced tracks, a prewritten calendar, and automatic increases by day. Their advantages are immediate comprehension, strong search intent, and low onboarding cost. Their limitation is that “progression” is often elapsed-time progression rather than user-performance progression.

### Exercise libraries and workout builders

These products offer choice and control. They can satisfy experienced users but often transfer programming responsibility back to beginners. Large catalog size does not solve “what should I do today?”

### Broad personalized-fitness subscriptions

These products offer polished onboarding, wide workout coverage, and “personalized” or “AI” positioning. Their breadth, cost, and opaque recommendation logic create room for a narrow product whose adaptation is easier to understand.

### Minimalist trackers and counters

These products demonstrate demand for speed, simplicity, and low friction. They generally lack structured progression, variation, recovery logic, and a long-term path.

### Strength-training trackers

Products focused on logged sets, records, and progressive overload show that visible performance history can motivate continued use. Their interaction model is often too manual for the target beginner and may not specialize in core-program balance.

## 4. User jobs

The target user is trying to:

- know what core workout to do today;
- fit it into a short available window;
- avoid routines that are obviously too easy or too hard;
- understand how to improve without designing a program;
- perform unfamiliar movements with confidence;
- resume after missed days without feeling that the plan is broken;
- see progress before visual body changes become obvious;
- avoid an untrustworthy paywall or advertising experience.

## 5. Pain-point synthesis

| Theme | Evidence status | User consequence | Product implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static schedules | Recurring qualitative theme | Plan ignores ability and daily reality | Generate after performance, not by day number |
| Sudden difficulty jumps | Observed examples and recurring theme | Failure, poor form, discouragement | Use small load changes and repeat/hold options |
| Repetition without purpose | Recurring qualitative theme | Boredom and perceived low value | Controlled variation with recent-exposure rules |
| Feedback has no effect | Recurring qualitative theme | “Personalized” claim feels false | Feedback must visibly alter state and later output |
| Program-end dead end | Observed example | User does not know how to continue | Continuous cycles without a terminal day 30 |
| Missed-day friction | Observed example and hypothesis | Calendar feels punitive or irrelevant | Recalculate from current state and elapsed recovery |
| Weak form guidance | Recurring qualitative theme | Low confidence and safety concern | Short demonstration plus critical cues |
| Limited settings persistence | Observed examples | Repeated annoyance interrupts workouts | Persist cue, tempo, and sound preferences |
| Broken/absent health integrations | Observed examples | Fragmented tracking | Defer integrations until the core loop is reliable |
| Aggressive ads/paywalls | Recurring qualitative theme | Trust loss and churn | Transparent, stable monetization |
| Purchase terms changed later | Observed examples | Strong betrayal response | Preserve entitlements and communicate terms clearly |

## 6. Behavioral insight

People do not inherently want to perform crunches. They want evidence that effort is producing progress. This supports progress-centered presentation: current capability, completed work, personal bests, and understandable changes to the next session.

Gamification concepts such as levels, streaks, heatmaps, missions, XP, and unlocks were discussed. They remain secondary. The MVP first proves that correct adaptation and visible progress create return behavior; it does not depend on a complete game economy.

## 7. Product opportunity

The opportunity is not “more exercises.” It is the combination of:

- a narrow, easily understood starting use case;
- real adaptation based on completion and perceived effort;
- controlled movement-pattern variety;
- conservative recovery and pain handling;
- short sessions that fit real schedules;
- visible explanations and progress;
- a simple interface with low operational cost.

This combination may be more credible than broad “AI coach” positioning because the user can observe the cause-and-effect loop.

## 8. User language and scientific language

Users understand labels such as upper abs, lower abs, obliques, deep core, and stability. The programming engine should not rely on those labels alone. It should balance movement patterns such as trunk flexion, anti-extension, anti-rotation, rotation, lateral stability, and hip-control contribution.

This dual vocabulary preserves accessibility without reducing program design to a misleading muscle map.

## 9. Trust and health considerations

Marketing must not imply that targeted core exercise alone reduces abdominal fat or guarantees visible abs within a fixed period. The product is a training and progression tool, not medical care or a body-composition guarantee.

Pain feedback is a stop/exclude signal. It does not authorize diagnosis or rehabilitation advice. Any medical or sports-science claims require expert review before publication.

## 10. Initial go-to-market implications

Discovery selected organic-first growth because a student solo founder is operating with near-zero budget and limited time:

- TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts, Reddit, and relevant communities;
- content about progress, consistency, form cues, and how adaptation works;
- shareable weekly progress cards as a later organic loop;
- closed beta recruitment before paid campaigns;
- a free closed beta that also tests willingness to pay and package preference transparently;
- paid acquisition only after retention is credible.

The exact channel mix, creative format, acquisition cost, and store keywords were not validated and must be tested rather than assumed.

## 11. Validation plan

### Problem interviews

Interview busy beginner/early-intermediate home exercisers. Confirm recent behavior rather than asking whether the idea sounds good:

- What did you do the last time a workout felt too hard?
- How did your last app decide what came next?
- What made you stop or miss sessions?
- What progress did you expect to see?
- Which exercise instructions did you actually use?

### Prototype tests

Test whether users can:

- understand the value proposition without explanation;
- select a time budget and start;
- replace a movement;
- submit effort and discomfort feedback;
- correctly explain why the next workout changed.

### Closed-beta evidence

Measure:

- onboarding completion;
- first-workout start and completion;
- feedback completion;
- second-workout return;
- workout replacements and reasons;
- pain flags;
- D1, D7, and D30 retention;
- perceived appropriateness of progression;
- support issues and algorithm overrides;
- paywall or upgrade intent without collecting payment during the free beta;
- stated willingness to pay compared with observed upgrade intent;
- package and price preference;
- operating cost and support time per beta user.

## 12. Research gaps intentionally left for execution

These are validation tasks, not invitations to restart product discovery:

- current competitor price and entitlement audit;
- commercial-license verification for every exercise asset;
- expert review of movement classifications, contraindications, and progression chains;
- interviews with the exact initial user segment;
- store-policy and health-disclaimer review;
- willingness-to-pay and packaging tests.
